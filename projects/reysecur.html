01 / THE CONTEXT

Usability changes when the work changes.

MailSecur is specialized mail-screening equipment. Operators use its interface to inspect images, adjust viewing settings, and take action on items that require attention. Those tasks happen in the context of physical equipment and workplace processes—not in the controlled conditions of a design mockup.

The existing interface already brought much of its critical functionality onto one screen. Our challenge was to preserve those strengths while identifying where icon choices, terminology, control behavior, and feedback could create unnecessary interpretation or mistakes.

THE DESIGN QUESTION

How might we make a specialized screening interface clearer, more predictable, and easier to operate in the conditions where it's actually used?

02 / RESEARCH APPROACH

Looking at the system from more than one angle.

Our assessment combined established usability principles, information from product demonstrations, and a review of competing inspection technologies. Each lens helped connect what happens on the screen with the context in which operators use the equipment.

01

Heuristic evaluation

Examined the interface against ten widely used usability guidelines, identifying clarity, feedback, control, and consistency issues.

02

Demonstration-informed evaluation

Used information from in-person and virtual demonstrations to identify potential operator pain points and practical workflow constraints.

03

Competitor analysis

Reviewed three competing inspection products to understand comparable functionality, audiences, and interaction patterns.

Research scope

The team's presentation describes these evaluation methods, but does not provide a participant count, timed usability results, or measured post-redesign outcomes. The findings below are presented qualitatively.

03 / RESEARCH TO DESIGN

Four areas where small details can have a big operational impact.

01 / RECOGNITION

Make controls recognizable.

WE IDENTIFIED

Icons did not always communicate their purpose clearly, and differing icon styles weakened consistency across the interface.

WE RECOMMENDED

Use familiar symbols, standardize icon styles and stroke weight, and apply color consistently. Reserve red as a meaningful cue for active recording.

02 / CLARITY

Say what the action actually does.

WE IDENTIFIED

Technical terminology, lengthy labels, and inconsistent contrast added reading and interpretation work.

WE RECOMMENDED

Use action-oriented language such as “Clear Screen,” strengthen contrast, and introduce a more deliberate hierarchy of text, buttons, and warnings.

03 / CONTROL

Make interaction patterns predictable.

WE IDENTIFIED

Controls for related functions sometimes worked differently, and available actions weren't always self-explanatory.

WE RECOMMENDED

Consolidate overlapping controls, standardize binary selections, and add contextual help and easier resetting of adjustments.

04 / OPERATOR REALITY

Design for gloves, feedback, and mistakes.

WE IDENTIFIED

Small touch targets, dropdown-heavy controls, and unclear states could introduce friction in gloved or demanding workflows.

WE RECOMMENDED

Increase target padding, consider large on-screen selections, and provide clear visual, audio, or haptic feedback where appropriate.

FROM RESEARCH TO RECOMMENDATIONS

Turning insights into practical design decisions.

My evaluation brought three priorities into focus: recognizable controls, larger touch targets designed with gloved operators in mind, and clear feedback for critical system actions. Here's how I would bring those recommendations into the interface.

04 / THE DESIGN RESPONSE

Research, translated into interface decisions.

The team translated research into a proposed interface concept featuring grouped controls, clearer action icons, larger on-screen selections, and consolidated image adjustments. The screen on the right is an illustrative portfolio refinement of the team's original interface concept, exploring more generous touch-target spacing and clearer control grouping. It has not been implemented or usability-tested.

05 / BEYOND THE SCREEN

Put the interface back into the work.

06 / CONTRIBUTION & OUTCOME

Good UX research doesn't stop at a finding.

It creates a basis for design decisions that can be explained, challenged, and validated.

MY ROLE

UX research and project lead

As project lead on our four-person graduate team, I coordinated our work and contributed to the heuristic evaluation, competitor analysis, research synthesis, and usability recommendations. The original team UI mockups were developed by teammates using our shared findings. The newer screen and environmental images shown here are subsequent AI-assisted portfolio visualizations, not deliverables validated with operators.

WHAT WE DELIVERED

An evaluated interface and proposed redesign

We delivered prioritized usability recommendations and a team interface concept. This case study does not claim an implemented redesign or measured improvements in task success, efficiency, or error reduction.

IF THIS MOVED FORWARD

What I would validate next

  • Can operators recognize core controls without relying on memorized icon meanings?
  • Are touch targets and selections reliable while wearing the gloves used in screening work?
  • Do warning and success states remain noticeable without disrupting the workflow?
THE TAKEAWAY

Good interface design isn't just about what looks clear on a screen. It's about what works for the person, in their real work enviroment.
